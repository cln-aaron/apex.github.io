03B // Keynote

Counter-Evolution:
Your Brain on AI.

A two-part keynote by Aaron Ang woven across both days — part wake-up call, part survival guide. Backed by cutting-edge research from MIT, UCLA, and the APA. This isn't a lecture. It's a mirror.

Day 1 Keynote

The Cognitive Debt You Don't Know You're Paying

Every time you let AI think for you, your brain quietly charges you interest.

MIT Media Lab researchers strapped EEG sensors on 54 students for four months. The result? People who regularly used AI chatbots showed measurably weaker neural activity during critical thinking and creative tasks — even when the AI wasn't there. They coined a term for it: cognitive debt. The AI spares you effort today but erodes your capacity tomorrow.

In a separate study, students given unrestricted ChatGPT access scored 11 points lower on retention tests than those who studied the traditional way. Not because they were less intelligent — but because their brains never had to do the heavy lifting. The information passed through them without sticking.

This is cognitive offloading — the same phenomenon that made us forget phone numbers when smartphones arrived, and weakened our sense of direction when GPS took over. But this time it's not just memory. It's your ability to reason, create, and think critically that's on the line.

Research Kosmyna et al. (2025), MIT Media Lab — "Your Brain on ChatGPT" Barcaui (2025) — ChatGPT as a Cognitive Crutch (Randomised Controlled Trial) Sparrow, Liu & Wegner (2011), Science — The Google Effect on Memory
Day 1 Deep Dive

Your Brain Is Still Under Construction

The prefrontal cortex — the part that makes you you — doesn't finish building until you're 25.

Right now, between ages 16 and 25, your brain is going through its most critical rewiring phase. The prefrontal cortex — responsible for judgement, planning, critical thinking, and impulse control — is still pruning, strengthening, and myelinating. Every hard problem you solve, every argument you construct, every creative struggle you push through literally shapes the architecture of your adult brain.

Here's the concern psychologists are raising: AI delivers consistently agreeable, pre-processed answers. It bypasses the cognitive friction your developing brain needs. And unlike adults who lose skills they once built — recoverable atrophy — young people who never build those circuits in the first place face what researchers now call cognitive foreclosure. As one psychologist put it: "You can't atrophy a muscle that was never built."

Research Arain et al. (2013), Neuropsychiatric Disease & Treatment — Maturation of the Adolescent Brain APA Health Advisory — AI and Adolescent Well-Being (2025) Psychology Today (2026) — Cognitive Foreclosure vs. Cognitive Atrophy

Day 2 Keynote

Counter-Evolution: How to Use AI Without Losing Yourself

The paradox: the best AI users are the ones who don't need AI.

Psychologist Daniel Kahneman split thinking into two systems: System 1 (fast, automatic, effortless) and System 2 (slow, deliberate, analytical). AI is essentially an infinite System 1 machine — it gives you the answer without the effort. The danger isn't using it. The danger is that your System 2 — the muscle that makes you capable of original thought — atrophies from disuse.

UCLA researcher Robert Bjork proved that desirable difficulties — struggle, friction, even confusion — are what make learning stick. Students who found it harder to learn retained twice as much as those who found it easy. AI removes the difficulty. Which means it removes the mechanism that builds durable knowledge.

But here's the flip side — and this is why you're at this bootcamp. Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development shows that the right kind of support, at the right time, accelerates growth. AI can be that support — if you use it as a scaffold that fades, not a crutch that stays. The difference between the person who thrives with AI and the person who's diminished by it comes down to one thing: intentionality.

Research Kahneman (2011) — Thinking, Fast and Slow (System 1 / System 2) Bjork & Bjork (2020), UCLA — Desirable Difficulties in Theory and Practice Vygotsky — Zone of Proximal Development; 2024 meta-analysis: scaffold fading (d=0.71) vs. constant support (d=0.32) Gerlich (2025) — AI Reliance and Critical Thinking in Youth (ages 17-25 most affected)
Day 2 Framework

The APEX Protocol: 5 Rules for AI-Augmented Thinking

Use AI harder. Think harder. Never confuse the two.

01Think First. Attempt the problem yourself before consulting AI. The struggle is the point — that's where your brain builds.
02Interrogate, Don't Accept. Every AI output is a draft, not a verdict. Verify. Question. Improve. Your critical lens is the value you bring.
03Fade the Scaffold. Use AI heavily when you're learning something new — then deliberately pull back as you get better. Dependence is the failure mode.
04Protect Deep Work. Carve out time to think without AI. Write without autocomplete. Solve without search. Flow requires sustained struggle.
05Own Your Output. If you can't explain it, defend it, or rebuild it without AI — you didn't learn it. You rented it.

What's new in 2026: attackers now use AI to automate at scale, and defenders have to run AI of their own just to keep up. Singapore, as a regional financial and tech hub, sits at the front of this arms race.

But here's the part that surprises most people — cybersecurity isn't just a career for hackers. It's a way of thinking. The hacker's instinct to ask "what happens if I break this?" transfers to product design, policy, finance, even content. And unlike almost every other high-paying field, ethical hacking lets you prove yourself before you get hired.
